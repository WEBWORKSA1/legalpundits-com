How Divorce Works: A Step-by-Step Guide

From filing the petition to the final decree: residency rules, no-fault grounds, property division, support and the timeline.

By the LegalPundits Editorial Team · Last reviewed November 2026 · 3 min read · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Every US state allows no-fault divorce; you don't need to prove wrongdoing.
  • Residency requirements decide where you can file.
  • Uncontested divorces are faster and far cheaper than litigated ones.
  • Property is divided under community-property or equitable-distribution rules depending on your state.

Step 1: Check residency and grounds

You file in a state where you or your spouse meets the residency requirement, which ranges from no minimum in a few states to six months or a year in many. All states allow no-fault divorce based on irreconcilable differences or an irretrievable breakdown; some also allow fault grounds such as adultery or cruelty, which can occasionally affect property or support.

Step 2: File the petition

One spouse (the petitioner) files a petition or complaint with the family court and pays a filing fee, which can usually be waived for low income. The petition states the grounds and what you're asking for: custody, support, property division and possibly a name change.

Step 3: Serve your spouse

The other spouse must receive formal notice. They then have a set time, often 20 to 30 days, to respond. If they don't, you may be able to obtain a default judgment.

Step 4: Temporary orders

Courts can issue temporary orders for custody, child support, spousal support and who stays in the home while the case proceeds.

Step 5: Financial disclosure

Both spouses must exchange financial information: income, assets, debts, retirement accounts and expenses. Hiding assets can lead to serious penalties.

Step 6: Negotiate or mediate

Most divorces settle. Mediation is common and is required in many courts for custody disputes. A signed marital settlement agreement can resolve everything.

Step 7: Trial (if needed)

Unresolved issues go to a judge. Trials add significant cost and time.

Step 8: Final judgment

The judge signs the decree. Many states impose a waiting period between filing and the final decree, from none to six months or more.

How property is divided

  • Community property states (Arizona, California, Idaho, Louisiana, Nevada, New Mexico, Texas, Washington and Wisconsin) generally split marital property equally.
  • Equitable distribution states divide marital property fairly, which isn't always equally, considering factors such as the length of the marriage and each spouse's earnings.

Separate property, such as assets owned before marriage, gifts and inheritances kept separate, usually stays with the original owner.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a divorce take?

An uncontested divorce can finish in a few months, subject to any state waiting period. Contested cases commonly take one to two years.

How much does a divorce cost?

Uncontested divorces can cost a few hundred dollars in fees. Contested divorces with lawyers on both sides often cost tens of thousands.

Do I need a lawyer for an uncontested divorce?

Not always, but having a lawyer review the settlement is wise when there are children, retirement accounts, a business or real estate.

When to call a lawyer

Hire a family lawyer if custody is disputed, there's domestic violence, your spouse controls the finances, there's a business or significant retirement assets, or your spouse already has a lawyer.
