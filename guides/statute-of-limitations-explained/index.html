Statutes of Limitations Explained: How Legal Deadlines Work

What a statute of limitations is, when the clock starts, how it can pause, and why missing it usually ends a case for good.

By the LegalPundits Editorial Team · Last reviewed November 2026 · 3 min read · How we write

Key takeaways

  • A statute of limitations is the legal deadline to file a lawsuit; miss it and the court will almost always dismiss the case.
  • Deadlines vary by state and by type of claim (injury, contract, property, malpractice).
  • The clock can start later or pause for minors, hidden injuries or a defendant who leaves the state.
  • Government claims often require a notice of claim within months, long before the lawsuit deadline.

What it is

A statute of limitations sets a time limit for bringing a lawsuit. The idea is fairness: evidence fades, witnesses forget, and people shouldn't face old claims forever. If you file after the deadline, the defendant can raise the statute as a defence and the case is usually thrown out, however strong it was.

Typical deadlines

These vary widely, but common US ranges are:

  • Personal injury: 1 to 6 years, with 2 or 3 years most common.
  • Written contracts: often 4 to 10 years.
  • Oral contracts: often 2 to 6 years.
  • Property damage: often 2 to 6 years.
  • Medical malpractice: often 1 to 3 years, usually with an outer limit (a statute of repose).
  • Wrongful death: usually measured from the date of death, often 1 to 3 years.

Criminal charges have their own limitations periods, and the most serious crimes, such as murder, often have none.

When the clock starts

The clock usually starts on the date of injury. Important exceptions:

  • Discovery rule. If an injury wasn't reasonably discoverable (for example, a surgical instrument left inside a patient), the clock may start when it was or should have been discovered.
  • Minors. Many states pause ("toll") the deadline until a child turns 18.
  • Mental incapacity can toll the deadline.
  • Defendant absent from the state or concealing themselves can toll it in some states.

Notice-of-claim traps

Suing a city, county, state agency or public hospital often requires a formal written notice within a short period, sometimes 30, 60, 90 or 180 days. Missing that notice can bar the claim even when the lawsuit deadline is years away.

How to protect yourself

Write down the date of the incident, check your state's deadline with our deadline finder, and talk to a lawyer well before the deadline; lawyers need time to investigate and draft. Use the court days calculator to count days precisely.

Frequently asked questions

Can a statute of limitations be extended?

Not by agreement after the fact in most cases, but tolling rules (minority, incapacity, discovery, absence) can delay or pause it. A lawyer can tell you whether any exception applies.

Does an insurance claim stop the clock?

No. Negotiating with an insurer does not pause the lawsuit deadline. If talks drag on, a lawsuit may need to be filed to protect the claim.

What happens if I miss the deadline?

The defendant can ask the court to dismiss, and courts almost always grant it unless an exception applies.

When to call a lawyer

If your deadline is less than six months away, the injury involves a government entity, or you were injured as a minor or discovered the harm late, contact an attorney now.
