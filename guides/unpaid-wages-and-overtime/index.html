Unpaid Wages and Overtime: Your Rights Under the FLSA

Who qualifies for overtime, how it's calculated, common wage theft tactics and how to recover unpaid pay.

By the LegalPundits Editorial Team · Last reviewed November 2026 · 3 min read · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Non-exempt employees must receive at least 1.5× their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek under federal law.
  • Job titles don't decide exemption; salary level and actual duties do.
  • Federal back-pay claims generally reach back 2 years, or 3 for willful violations; many states allow more.
  • Keep your own record of hours worked.

The federal baseline

The Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) sets the federal minimum wage (currently $7.25 an hour, though most states and many cities set higher minimums) and requires overtime pay of at least one and a half times the regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a single workweek. Some states, such as California, also require daily overtime.

Exempt or non-exempt?

Being paid a salary doesn't automatically make you exempt. The common white-collar exemptions (executive, administrative, professional) generally require both a minimum salary and specific job duties. Misclassification is one of the most common wage violations: calling someone a "manager" who mostly performs routine work, or treating employees as independent contractors.

Common forms of wage theft

  • Off-the-clock work: pre-shift setup, post-shift cleanup, security checks, logging in to computers.
  • Automatic meal-break deductions when breaks are interrupted or skipped.
  • Averaging hours across two weeks to avoid overtime (the FLSA looks at each workweek separately).
  • Excluding bonuses or shift differentials from the regular rate used to calculate overtime.
  • Unlawful deductions for uniforms, tools or shortages that push pay below minimum wage.
  • Withheld final paycheques; many states set strict final-pay deadlines.

How to recover unpaid wages

  1. Document. Write down your hours daily, keep pay stubs and save schedules.
  2. Raise it in writing with your employer or HR if you feel safe doing so. Retaliation for wage complaints is illegal.
  3. File a complaint with the US Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division or your state labor agency, which can investigate at no cost.
  4. Sue. Successful FLSA plaintiffs can recover back pay, an equal amount in liquidated (double) damages, and attorney fees, which is why many wage lawyers take these cases on contingency.

The federal limitations period is generally two years, three for willful violations. State claims can reach back further.

Frequently asked questions

Can my employer give me comp time instead of overtime?

Private employers generally cannot substitute comp time for overtime pay for non-exempt employees. Public-sector rules differ.

Do salaried employees get overtime?

Some do. Salary alone does not make you exempt; your salary level and duties must meet an exemption test.

Can I be fired for complaining about pay?

Retaliation for wage complaints is prohibited under federal law and many state laws.

When to call a lawyer

Consider a wage-and-hour attorney if many co-workers are affected (a possible collective action), you've been misclassified as a contractor or exempt, or your employer retaliated.
