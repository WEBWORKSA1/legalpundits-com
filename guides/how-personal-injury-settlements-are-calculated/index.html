How Personal Injury Settlements Are Calculated

Economic damages, pain and suffering, the multiplier method, comparative fault and policy limits: how injury claims are actually valued.

By the LegalPundits Editorial Team · Last reviewed November 2026 · 3 min read · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Settlements combine economic damages (bills, lost wages) with non-economic damages (pain, suffering, lost enjoyment).
  • Adjusters often start with a multiplier of medical costs, commonly between 1.5 and 5 depending on severity.
  • Your share of fault and the at-fault driver's policy limit can shrink a claim more than any other factor.
  • Documentation drives value: consistent treatment records are worth more than a dramatic story.

The two buckets of damages

Economic damages are losses with a receipt: medical bills, future treatment, prescriptions, physical therapy, lost wages, lost earning capacity, property damage and out-of-pocket costs such as transport to appointments.

Non-economic damages compensate for things without a price tag: physical pain, emotional distress, anxiety, sleep loss, scarring and the loss of activities you used to enjoy. Some states cap non-economic damages in certain cases, especially medical malpractice.

The multiplier method

Insurance adjusters and their software frequently estimate non-economic damages by multiplying medical expenses by a factor:

  • 1.5–2 for soft-tissue injuries that heal within weeks.
  • 2–3 for injuries needing months of treatment or injections.
  • 3–5 for fractures, surgery, permanent limitations or long-term pain.

The result is added to economic damages to produce a claim value. The per diem method is an alternative that assigns a daily dollar amount (often tied to daily wages) for each day of recovery.

What reduces a claim

  • Comparative fault. In pure comparative-fault states, your award drops by your percentage of fault. In modified comparative-fault states, you recover nothing once your share reaches 50% or 51%, depending on the state. Alabama, Maryland, North Carolina, Virginia and Washington, D.C. still use contributory negligence, where even slight fault can bar recovery.
  • Policy limits. If the at-fault driver carries a $25,000 limit, that is often the practical ceiling unless they have significant personal assets or you carry underinsured-motorist coverage.
  • Treatment gaps and pre-existing conditions. Insurers argue that gaps mean you recovered, and that pain came from an older injury.
  • Liens. Health insurers, Medicare, Medicaid and hospitals may be repaid from your settlement.

A worked example

Medical bills of $12,000, lost wages of $3,000 and a severity multiplier of 2.5 give: $12,000 × 2.5 = $30,000 non-economic, plus $15,000 economic = $45,000. If you were 20% at fault in a pure comparative state, the value falls to $36,000. If you hired a lawyer at 33.3%, your gross fee is about $12,000 before costs and liens. Try your own numbers with the settlement estimator and the net recovery calculator.

Why lawyered claims often settle higher

Lawyers gather complete medical evidence, calculate future costs, identify extra insurance (umbrella policies, employer liability, underinsured-motorist coverage) and can credibly threaten a lawsuit. That said, small claims with low medical bills may not justify a fee, so a free consultation is the right first step.

Frequently asked questions

What is the average personal injury settlement?

There is no meaningful single average because values depend on injuries, fault, venue and insurance limits. Minor soft-tissue claims often resolve for a few thousand dollars, while surgery cases can reach six or seven figures.

Are personal injury settlements taxable?

Under US federal tax law, compensation for physical injuries or physical sickness is generally excluded from income, but interest, punitive damages and some emotional-distress awards can be taxable. Confirm with a tax professional.

How long does a settlement take?

Simple claims can settle within a few months of finishing treatment. Claims that go into litigation commonly take one to three years.

When to call a lawyer

Get a lawyer's evaluation if your medical bills are significant, you have lasting symptoms, fault is disputed, or the insurer's offer doesn't cover your bills and lost income.
