Workers' Compensation Basics: Benefits, Deadlines and Denials

What workers' comp covers, how to report an injury, the benefits available and what to do if your claim is denied.

By the LegalPundits Editorial Team · Last reviewed November 2026 · 3 min read · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Workers' comp is generally no-fault: you can receive benefits even if the accident was your mistake.
  • Report your injury to your employer in writing as soon as possible; state deadlines can be short.
  • In exchange for benefits you usually can't sue your employer, but you may be able to sue a negligent third party.

How the system works

Workers' compensation is a state-run insurance system. Employers buy coverage (or self-insure), and injured employees receive benefits regardless of fault. The trade-off: workers' comp is usually the exclusive remedy against your employer, so you can't sue them for pain and suffering. Federal employees, longshore workers and some other groups are covered by federal programs instead.

What's covered

  • Injuries from accidents at work or while performing job duties (including work travel).
  • Repetitive-stress injuries such as carpal tunnel.
  • Occupational diseases caused by workplace exposure.
  • Aggravation of a pre-existing condition by work.

Injuries during a commute, horseplay, intoxication or intentional self-injury are commonly excluded, with state-specific exceptions.

Benefits

  • Medical care related to the injury, typically with no co-pay.
  • Temporary disability wage replacement, often around two-thirds of your average weekly wage up to a state maximum.
  • Permanent disability benefits when an injury causes lasting impairment.
  • Vocational rehabilitation in some states.
  • Death benefits for dependants.

Steps after a work injury

  1. Get medical care, and tell the provider the injury happened at work.
  2. Report the injury to your supervisor in writing. Deadlines range from a few days to about 30 days in many states.
  3. File the state claim form if your employer doesn't; there's a separate, longer deadline for this.
  4. Keep copies of everything and track missed work.

If your claim is denied

Denials commonly cite late reporting, "not work-related" or a pre-existing condition. You have the right to appeal through your state's workers' compensation board, usually starting with a hearing before an administrative judge. Many comp lawyers work for a fee capped by state law and paid from your award.

Third-party claims

If someone other than your employer caused the injury (a negligent driver, a defective machine manufacturer, a subcontractor), you may have a separate personal injury claim that can include pain and suffering.

Frequently asked questions

Can I be fired for filing a workers' comp claim?

Retaliating against an employee for filing a claim is illegal in most states, and it can create a separate legal claim.

Can I choose my own doctor?

It depends on your state. Some let you choose; others require an employer-designated provider at least initially.

Do I pay taxes on workers' comp benefits?

Workers' compensation benefits are generally not taxable for federal income tax purposes.

When to call a lawyer

Talk to a workers' comp attorney if your claim is denied, your benefits stop, you're offered a lump-sum settlement, or your injury may be permanent.
