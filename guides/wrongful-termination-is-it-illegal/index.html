Wrongful Termination: When Is Firing Someone Illegal?

At-will employment, the illegal reasons for firing, retaliation, and the deadlines for filing a discrimination charge.

By the LegalPundits Editorial Team · Last reviewed November 2026 · 3 min read · How we write

Key takeaways

  • Most US jobs are at-will: you can be fired for almost any reason, but not an illegal one.
  • Illegal reasons include discrimination, retaliation for protected activity, and firing in violation of a contract or public policy.
  • Federal discrimination claims usually require filing an EEOC charge within 180 days (300 in many states).
  • Save texts, emails, reviews and a timeline before you lose access to work systems.

At-will employment and its limits

In every state except Montana, private employment is presumed at-will. An employer can end the job without a reason, and you can quit without notice. But at-will doesn't mean "anything goes". A firing is unlawful when the real reason is one the law forbids.

Illegal reasons to fire

  • Discrimination based on race, colour, religion, sex (including pregnancy, sexual orientation and gender identity), national origin, age (40+), disability or genetic information under federal law; many states add more protected traits.
  • Retaliation for complaining about discrimination or harassment, reporting safety violations, filing a workers' compensation claim, taking protected family or medical leave, or participating in an investigation.
  • Whistleblowing about fraud or illegal conduct, protected by many federal and state statutes.
  • Breach of contract, where a written contract or a clear policy promises termination only for cause.
  • Public policy violations, such as firing someone for serving on a jury or refusing to break the law.

Evidence that matters

Timing is often the strongest clue: a firing shortly after a complaint, leave request or injury report. Inconsistent explanations, glowing reviews followed by sudden "performance issues", and comparisons with co-workers outside your protected group who were treated better are also powerful.

Before your access ends, save personal copies of performance reviews, commendations, relevant emails and texts (only your own and those you're entitled to keep), and write a dated timeline.

Deadlines

To sue under most federal anti-discrimination laws you must first file a charge with the EEOC, generally within 180 calendar days of the firing, extended to 300 days where a state or local agency enforces a similar law. State-law deadlines differ. Retaliation claims under some whistleblower laws can have deadlines as short as 30 days.

Severance agreements

Severance offers usually require you to release legal claims. Read carefully: if you're 40 or older, federal law gives you at least 21 days to consider a release and 7 days to revoke it after signing (45 days to consider in group layoffs).

Frequently asked questions

Can I be fired without a reason?

In an at-will state, generally yes, unless the real reason is illegal or you have a contract requiring cause.

What is constructive discharge?

When an employer makes conditions so intolerable that a reasonable person would quit, the resignation can be treated as a firing for legal purposes.

Can I get unemployment if I was fired?

Often yes, unless you were fired for serious misconduct. Apply promptly; eligibility is decided by your state agency.

When to call a lawyer

Contact an employment lawyer before signing a severance agreement, if you were fired soon after a complaint, injury or leave request, or if your EEOC deadline is approaching.
